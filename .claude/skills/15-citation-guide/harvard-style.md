# Harvard Referencing Style

Harvard is an author-date citation system widely used in UK, Australian, and some European universities. Unlike APA and Chicago, there is no single official Harvard manual -- institutions often publish their own Harvard guides. This file covers the most widely accepted conventions.

## Why Harvard Style Matters

If you study at a UK or Australian university, Harvard is likely the default citation style for your courses. Because there is no single standard, always check your institution's specific Harvard guide for any local variations.

## In-Text Citations

### Basic Format

**One author**: (Hattie 2009) or Hattie (2009) states that...

**Two authors**: (Brown and Lee 2014) or (Brown & Lee 2014) -- conventions vary by institution; check which is preferred.

**Three or more authors**: (Brown et al. 2021) from the first citation.

**Page number**: (Hattie 2009, p. 83) or (Hattie 2009, pp. 83-85)

**Multiple works**: (Biggs 2011; Hattie 2009; Vygotsky 1978) -- semicolons, alphabetical or chronological per your institution's guide.

**Multiple works by same author**: (Hattie 2009, 2012) or (Hattie 2012a, 2012b) if published in the same year.

**No author**: (World Bank 2025) -- use the organization name.

**No date**: (Smith n.d.)

**Secondary citation** (citing a source you found cited in another source):
(Vygotsky 1978, cited in Hattie and Timperley 2007)

**Note**: Always try to find the original source. Use secondary citations only when the original is unavailable.

## Reference List

The reference list appears at the end of the paper under the heading "References" or "Reference List." Entries are arranged alphabetically by the first author's surname.

### Journal Article

> Surname, Initials. (Year) 'Title of article', _Journal Name_, Volume(Issue), pp. Page range. doi:xxxxx.

**Example**:

> Hattie, J. and Timperley, H. (2007) 'The power of feedback', _Review of Educational Research_, 77(1), pp. 81-112. doi:10.3102/003465430298487.

**Note**: Some Harvard guides use single quotes around article titles; others do not. Some capitalize the article title; others use sentence case. Follow your institution's guide.

### Book

> Surname, Initials. (Year) _Title of Book_. Edition. Place of publication: Publisher.

**Example**:

> Biggs, J. and Tang, C. (2011) _Teaching for Quality Learning at University_. 4th edn. Maidenhead: Open University Press.

### Edited Book Chapter

> Surname, Initials. (Year) 'Title of chapter', in Editor Surname, Initials. (ed.) _Title of Book_. Place: Publisher, pp. Page range.

**Example**:

> Nuthall, G. (2011) 'The hidden lives of learners', in James, M. and Pollard, A. (eds.) _International Handbook of Educational Research_. Vol. 1. London: Routledge, pp. 139-158.

### Working Paper

> Surname, Initials. (Year) _Title of Paper_. Working Paper No. xxx. Institution. Available at: URL (Accessed: Day Month Year).

**Example**:

> Mollick, E. and Mollick, L. (2023) _Using AI to Implement Effective Teaching Strategies in Classrooms_. EdWorkingPaper No. 23-1. Providence, RI: Annenberg Institute at Brown University. Available at: https://doi.org/10.26300/example (Accessed: 1 March 2026).

### Institutional/Government Report

> Organization Name (Year) _Title of Report_. Place: Publisher. Available at: URL (Accessed: Day Month Year).

**Example**:

> United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (2024) _Global Education Monitoring Report: Technology in Education_. Paris: UNESCO. Available at: https://www.unesco.org/gem-report (Accessed: 10 March 2026).

### Website

> Author or Organization (Year) _Title of Webpage_. Available at: URL (Accessed: Day Month Year).

**Example**:

> American Psychological Association (2023) _How to Cite Generative AI in APA Style_. Available at: https://apastyle.apa.org/blog/how-to-cite-chatgpt (Accessed: 15 March 2026).

### Newspaper Article

> Surname, Initials. (Year) 'Title of article', _Newspaper Name_, Day Month, p. Page or Available at: URL (Accessed: Day Month Year).

**Example**:

> Carey, B. (2014) 'Studying for exams? Forget what you know about good study habits', _The New York Times_, 4 September. Available at: https://www.nytimes.com/2014/09/07/magazine/studying-for-exams.html (Accessed: 15 March 2026).

### Dataset

> Organization (Year) _Title of Dataset_ [Dataset]. Available at: URL (Accessed: Day Month Year).

**Example**:

> OECD (2024) _Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022 Results_ [Dataset]. OECD iLibrary. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1787/example (Accessed: 1 March 2026).

## Key Differences: Harvard vs. APA 7th vs. Chicago AD

| Feature                  | Harvard                | APA 7th                  | Chicago AD                      |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| Article title format     | Often in single quotes | No quotes, sentence case | In double quotes, headline case |
| In-text connector        | "and" or "&" (varies)  | "&"                      | "and"                           |
| Page abbreviation        | p. / pp.               | p. / pp.                 | Comma only                      |
| "Available at" for URLs  | Yes                    | No                       | No                              |
| "Accessed" date required | Yes (for online)       | No                       | Recommended                     |
| Edition abbreviation     | "edn."                 | "ed."                    | "ed."                           |
| DOI format               | doi:xxxxx              | https://doi.org/xxxxx    | https://doi.org/xxxxx.          |
| Publisher location       | Required               | Not required             | Required                        |

## Common Mistakes

1. **Assuming all Harvard guides are identical**: They are not. Always check your university's specific Harvard guide. Variations exist in punctuation, capitalization, and formatting.

2. **Omitting "Available at" and "Accessed" for online sources**: Harvard style requires both the URL and the access date for online sources.

3. **Inconsistent use of "and" vs. "&"**: Pick one based on your institution's guide and use it consistently throughout.

4. **Forgetting "edn." for editions**: Harvard typically uses "edn." rather than "ed." for edition. Check your institution's guide.

5. **Not including page numbers for direct quotes**: In-text citations of direct quotes must include page numbers: (Hattie 2009, p. 83).

6. **Mixing Harvard with APA formatting**: These styles look similar but differ in key details (quotes around titles, DOI format, accessed dates). Do not mix them.
