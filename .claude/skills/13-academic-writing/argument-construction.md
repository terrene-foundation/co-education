# Argument Construction

Academic writing is not about expressing opinions -- it is about constructing rigorous arguments supported by evidence. Every claim you make needs backing, every counterargument needs addressing, and every logical step needs to be explicit.

## Why Argument Construction Matters

A paper that asserts a conclusion without evidence is no different from a blog post. Academic writing requires that you build a chain of reasoning from premises to conclusions, with each link supported by data, theory, or prior research. Strong arguments are what separate a first-class paper from a mediocre one.

## The Claim-Evidence-Warrant Model

Every academic argument has three components:

### 1. Claim (What you assert)

A specific, falsifiable statement.

**Strong claim**: "Students who receive structured feedback on drafts submit higher-quality final papers, as measured by rubric scores on argument and evidence."

**Weak claim**: "Feedback is important for student learning."

### 2. Evidence (What supports your claim)

Data, statistical results, citations to prior research, or logical reasoning.

**Types of evidence in academic research**:

- **Empirical**: Regression coefficients, experimental results, statistical tests
- **Theoretical**: Predictions from established models or frameworks
- **Prior literature**: Published findings from peer-reviewed journals
- **Case-based**: Specific real-world examples (supporting, not substituting for, systematic evidence)

### 3. Warrant (Why the evidence supports the claim)

The logical connection between the evidence and the claim. This is the most commonly omitted component.

**Example with all three components**:

> **Claim**: "Retrieval practice produces stronger long-term retention than re-reading."
>
> **Evidence**: "Roediger and Karpicke (2006) find that students who took practice tests recalled 61% of material one week later, compared to 40% for those who restudied. Our own follow-up assessment confirms this: the coefficient on retrieval-practice condition is 0.21 (p < 0.01), indicating that the effect persists across different subject domains."
>
> **Warrant**: "If retrieval practice were no more effective than passive review, we would expect no systematic difference in delayed recall. The statistically and practically significant positive coefficient suggests that actively reconstructing knowledge strengthens memory traces in ways that re-reading does not, consistent with the testing effect account of Roediger and Karpicke (2006)."

### Worked Example: Building a Full Argument

**Research question**: Does class size affect student learning outcomes in primary school?

**Paragraph structure**:

> "Smaller class sizes are associated with higher student achievement in the early years of primary school [**claim**]. Krueger (1999), analysing the STAR experiment, finds that students randomly assigned to smaller classes scored 0.22 standard deviations higher on standardised tests than those in regular-sized classes [**evidence**]. This result is consistent with the theoretical prediction that teachers in smaller classes can provide more individualised attention, enabling them to identify and address each student's specific misconceptions [**warrant**]. The effect persists after controlling for school, teacher experience, and prior attainment, suggesting it is not driven by observable selection differences [**strengthening the warrant**]."

## Counterarguments

Addressing counterarguments strengthens your paper by showing that you have considered alternative explanations.

### Structure for Addressing Counterarguments

1. **Acknowledge**: State the counterargument fairly (do not create a straw man)
2. **Evaluate**: Assess the strength of the counterargument
3. **Respond**: Explain why your argument holds despite the counterargument

**Worked Example**:

> "One might argue that the improvement in test scores following retrieval practice simply reflects greater time-on-task rather than any special benefit of testing itself [**acknowledge**]. Under this view, students who took practice tests merely spent more total time engaging with the material. However, this explanation predicts that equating study time between conditions should eliminate the difference [**evaluate**]. Studies that hold total study time constant still find a significant recall advantage for retrieval practice over re-reading, which is inconsistent with a pure time-on-task account and more consistent with the memory-consolidation mechanism proposed by Roediger and Karpicke (2006) [**respond**]."

### Alternative Explanations in Academic Research

Research papers must typically address the most plausible competing explanations:

| Your Hypothesis         | Common Alternative                    | How to Address                                         |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Causal effect of X on Y | Selection (who experiences X differs) | Use random assignment, matching, or natural experiment |
| Treatment effect        | Placebo effect or demand effects      | Include an active control condition; use blinding      |
| Conceptual explanation  | Methodological artefact               | Replicate with different instruments or measures       |
| Generalised pattern     | Sample-specific result                | Test in additional contexts; conduct a meta-analysis   |

## Logical Fallacies to Avoid

### 1. Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc (After this, therefore because of this)

"Test scores rose after the new curriculum was introduced. Therefore, the new curriculum caused the improvement."

**Problem**: Correlation is not causation. Scores may have risen for unrelated reasons (maturation, regression to the mean, concurrent interventions).

**Fix**: Use a control group or natural experiment to isolate the effect of the curriculum.

### 2. Survivorship Bias

"Programmes that have been running for 10+ years report strong outcomes."

**Problem**: This ignores programmes that were discontinued due to poor results. The survivors are not representative of the full population of programmes.

**Fix**: Include discontinued or failed cases in your sample when making claims about a broader population.

### 3. Cherry-Picking

"Our model fits the data well" (after testing 20 model specifications on the same dataset).

**Problem**: With enough attempts, any model can fit a dataset by chance. This is data snooping.

**Fix**: Use out-of-sample testing or pre-registration. Report all specifications you tested, not just the one that worked.

### 4. Appeal to Authority

"This must be true because a prominent researcher found it."

**Problem**: Even respected scholars can be wrong, and findings may not generalise to different samples, contexts, or time periods.

**Fix**: Evaluate the evidence on its merits. Replicate with your own data and context.

### 5. False Dichotomy

"Either this intervention works or it is completely ineffective."

**Problem**: These are extremes. Effects exist on a continuum, and partial or conditional effects are common.

**Fix**: Present the full range of possibilities and let the evidence characterise the magnitude and conditions of any effect.

### 6. Ecological Fallacy

"Countries with higher average literacy rates have higher life expectancy, so individuals who read more must live longer."

**Problem**: Aggregate-level relationships do not necessarily hold at the individual level.

**Fix**: Match the level of analysis to the level of your claim.

## Building Paragraph-Level Arguments

Each paragraph in an academic paper should follow this template:

1. **Topic sentence**: State the point of the paragraph (one clear idea)
2. **Supporting evidence**: Data, citations, or reasoning
3. **Explanation**: Connect the evidence to the point
4. **Transition**: Link to the next paragraph

**Example**:

> "Active learning strategies produce greater conceptual understanding than passive lecture formats [**topic sentence**]. Freeman et al. (2014) report in a meta-analysis of 225 studies that students in active learning courses scored 6% higher on examinations and were 1.5 times less likely to fail [**evidence**]. This advantage arises because active formats require students to retrieve, apply, and discuss ideas rather than simply receive them, strengthening both encoding and retention [**explanation**]. However, the benefit depends on task design and instructor scaffolding, which we examine in the next section [**transition**]."

## Strength of Claims

Match the strength of your language to the strength of your evidence.

| Evidence Strength                                            | Appropriate Language                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Strong causal (randomised experiment, natural experiment)    | "X causes Y" / "X leads to Y"                                          |
| Moderate (instrumental variables, difference-in-differences) | "X is associated with Y" / "The evidence suggests X affects Y"         |
| Correlational (regression without endogeneity controls)      | "X is correlated with Y" / "We observe a relationship between X and Y" |
| Theoretical (model prediction without empirical test)        | "The model predicts that X should lead to Y"                           |
| Speculative (reasoning without formal model or data)         | "It is plausible that X might affect Y"                                |

## Common Mistakes

1. **Asserting without evidence**: "Obviously, practice improves performance." Nothing is obvious in academic writing -- cite supporting research or show your data.

2. **Ignoring counterarguments**: Failing to address obvious alternative explanations makes your paper look one-sided and weakens your credibility.

3. **Overstating results**: Saying "our results prove" when you should say "our results suggest" or "our results are consistent with."

4. **Confusing statistical and practical significance**: A coefficient with p < 0.001 that represents a negligible real-world effect is statistically significant but practically meaningless.

5. **Straw man counterarguments**: Presenting a weak version of the opposing view and then demolishing it. Address the strongest version of the counterargument.

6. **Circular reasoning**: "This approach works because it produces better outcomes, and it produces better outcomes because this approach works." Break the circle with independent evidence.
